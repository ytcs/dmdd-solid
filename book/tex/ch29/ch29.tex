\chapter{Recoils, transport and cascades}\label{chap:29}

What a detector records after an energy deposit is decided in three stages. A struck atom leaves the region where the force constants of \cref{sec:5.2} apply and may end as a vacancy and an interstitial that store several eV. A hot electron multiplies into electron--hole pairs by impact ionization while it loses energy to phonons. The phonons and charges that result then cross the crystal, over microseconds, before a sensor absorbs them. \Cref{chap:18,chap:19,chap:20} derive the physics of these stages. The numbers they need come from simulations that follow many trajectories: the displacement threshold and the stored energy from molecular dynamics, the collection kernels from a transport Monte Carlo, the pair energy and the Fano factor from a cascade Monte Carlo. Each simulation is followed through silicon.

\section*{Summary}

Recoil molecular dynamics integrates Newton's equations on the Born--Oppenheimer surface, with classical nuclei and electrons that never take energy from them, using a machine-learned potential trained on density-functional cells and spliced to the Ziegler--Biersack--Littmark repulsion at short range. The displacement threshold is the lowest crossing of the defect probability, scanned over more than 1000 directions, and the stored energy is read after a quench, since \SI{1}{K} of residual heat in 4096 atoms adds \SI{0.53}{eV}. The transport Monte Carlo samples the linear Boltzmann equation for phonons and carriers with lowest-order rates: isotope scattering and anharmonic decay for phonons, one-phonon emission through a deformation potential for carriers. It returns collection kernels that are convolved with any event, and the closure of energy, charge, rates and time step costs nothing and catches the most common errors. The cascade Monte Carlo takes impact ionization from the imaginary part of the $GW$ self-energy on quasiparticle bands, and phonon emission from the electron--phonon vertex of DFPT. Its benchmark in silicon is the single-parameter model, which, tuned to the measured pair energy of \SI{3.749}{eV}, predicts a Fano factor of 0.114 against the measured $0.119 \pm 0.002$. A first-principles cascade has no fitted constant, so matching $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eh}}$ tests its kernels and $F_{\mathrm{eh}}$ is a second, independent test.
