\chapter*{Conventions and units}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Conventions and units}
We use natural units, $\hbar = c = k_{\mathrm{B}} = 1$, unless stated otherwise, and quote physical values in conventional units with SI prefixes.

A widely used formula from the literature appears in a gray box, a Limit Check, that plots it against the book's more complete result. A Limit Check has three parts: the formula, a plot, and one sentence. The formula is written in the book's notation, and a one-line citation names the equation of the source where it was first given. The plot compares the formula with a fuller result for the same quantity, computed in the book from a crystal calculation or from a model that keeps the physics the formula drops, or measured. Most boxes plot the ratio of the two, so that the formula is the horizontal line at one; some plot both curves, when their shapes matter more than their ratio. The horizontal axis carries the \emph{controlling parameter}, the variable along which the relative difference of the two runs from negligible at one end of the axis to order one at the other. The \emph{leading correction}, the first term of that difference in the controlling parameter, is drawn as a third curve, usually dotted or dashed. The gray band marks the values of the controlling parameter at which the formula agrees with the fuller result, to a tolerance stated in the caption, usually \SI{10}{\percent}, and the caption prints the edge of the band. In \cref{lim:1.3a} the free-recoil formula $\omega = q^2/2M$ is compared with a nucleus bound in an Einstein potential, against the recoil energy in units of the phonon energy, and the leading correction is the rms width $(E_R\omega_0)^{1/2}$ of the deposit. The sentence under the plot says what breaks down outside the band, and the steps from the book's general result to the formula are derived in the running text next to the box.

Inside the gray band the formula is established to the stated tolerance, and that statement is as strong as the fuller result. Where the fuller result is a model, the edge of the band is an estimate of where the formula fails, not a bound. Where nothing is shaded, the formula fails over the whole plotted range, and the box gives the size of the failure instead. Some boxes compare two published forms of one result, or test a sum rule; they are read in the same way, with the exact relation as the fuller result. \Cref{app:F} lists the most used of these formulas with their source equations and the sizes of their leading corrections.
