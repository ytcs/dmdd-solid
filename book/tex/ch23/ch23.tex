\chapter{Density-functional theory}\label{chap:23}

A dark-matter rate in silicon needs a few dozen numbers about the crystal: a gap good to tens of meV, a loss function out to \SI{100}{eV}, density vertices at momentum transfers of tens of keV, electron--phonon couplings on grids of $10^9$ points, and the energy a struck atom leaves behind. Almost none of them is measured everywhere it is needed. Each is computed by a method with its own approximation, numerical parameters and typical failure. \Crefrange{chap:23}{chap:29} follow each method through one calculation, almost always of silicon, so that a number in the text can be traced to what it neglects and to the check that would expose it. \Crefrange{chap:31}{chap:33} go the other way and specify, quantity by quantity, the calculation behind each number a rate uses, with its inputs, its method and its convergence targets.

At the base point of \cref{sec:8.5} (clamped nuclei, independent particles, no phonons) the calculation is the density-functional theory of \cref{sec:3.4}. The bands and wavefunctions behind computed dark-matter rates come from it, and the gap is its best-known failure: a density-functional calculation of silicon in the local-density approximation puts the gap at \SI{0.52}{eV}, less than half the measured \SI{1.17}{eV}. The computed gap would put the reach of a silicon detector at \SI{0.15}{MeV} instead of \SI{0.33}{MeV} (\cref{we:3-mmin-gap}). The Hartree--Fock equations and the theorems of Hohenberg and Kohn behind \cref{sec:3.4} are proved first; then a Kohn--Sham calculation of silicon is followed through its numerical parameters to a corrected gap.

\section*{Summary}

The Hartree--Fock equations are the minimum condition for the energy of a Slater determinant, and in a crystal Koopmans' theorem makes their bands the removal and addition energies of the Hartree--Fock state; in the electron gas they take closed form. The theorems of Hohenberg and Kohn and the Kohn--Sham equations of \cref{sec:3.4} follow from the constrained search of Levy. A measured gap already contains the zero-point renormalization, and a scissor to it must not add that renormalization a second time.

Every numerical parameter of the calculation is converged on the quantity that is used, not on the total energy: the $8^3$ grid that converges the energy of silicon to \SI{2}{meV} per atom leaves $\varepsilon_\infty$ nine percent too high. A scissor, the hybrid functional HSE and a one-shot $G_0W_0$ calculation give the silicon gap to about \SI{0.1}{eV} of one another. That spread and the screening ($\varepsilon_\infty$ of 13.0 computed against 11.7 measured) are the theory uncertainties that this chapter leaves in an electronic rate of silicon.
